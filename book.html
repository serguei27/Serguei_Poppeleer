Who it's for

Written for the person accountable for delivery.

Not for theorists. For the operator in the room when the site or supplier blows the plan.

For you if you are…

  • Project and programme directors running cross-border programmes — energy, infrastructure, real estate, industrial
  • Commercial and contracts managers who need governance that holds under site pressure, not just in the steering deck
  • Country directors and operations leads delivering in frontier markets, where imported playbooks fail first
  • Anyone who has asked "how did we not see this coming?" — and doesn't want to ask it again

This book is not for everyone.

  • People who believe more governance templates will fix delivery
  • Consultants looking for a methodology to repackage
  • Teams who think a traffic-light status report is a control system
  • Anyone who thinks frontier markets are "standard delivery with more bureaucracy"
  • Executives who want a book to hand to their team without reading it themselves
Inside the book

Five disciplines. One system.

Each chapter is one control. Read it, apply it, move to the next. No theory without a tool. No tool without a real-project proof.

1

CONTAIN

Scope control

Turn fuzzy requirements into one written scope — clear assumptions, acceptance criteria, explicit out-of-scope items.

Close "was that included?" for good.
2

COMMAND

One plan

Replace deck governance with one operating plan that matches how the site really runs, week to week.

Two plans means no plan.
3

CLOSE

Decision discipline

Decision owners, deadlines and consequences — so open items close before they cascade into delays and claims.

An open decision is a delay in waiting.
4

CONFIRM

Vendor verification

Rebuild supplier commitments around capacity, lead times and proof of readiness.

"The supplier promised the date." A promise is not a commitment.
5

CONTROL

Tower cadence

A fixed weekly rhythm — same inputs, same meeting, same outputs — so slippage is caught in week one, not month three.

A control tower does not motivate aircraft to land. It creates control.
Sneak peek

From the introduction.

Introduction · Projects don't fail

I have watched the same thing happen across more than thirty countries, on four continents.

The governance pack is clean. Stage gates signed off. Risk log updated. Steering deck submitted on time. And the project is drifting. Vendors missing dates. Scope expanding quietly. Weekly meetings that have become a recurring argument about what was actually agreed.

Projects don't fail. The systems around them do. Frontier markets just expose it faster.

Every failure I have seen — from a misaligned bridge approach in a Central African forest to a stalled EPC negotiation in Europe — had the same root cause. Not bad people. Not bad intentions. A gap between what the governance system said was true and what was actually happening in the field.

This book is about closing that gap. Not with more templates. Not with harder reporting. With five decisions that change what happens on site next week.

Controls that don't match reality are just documentation. No-surprises delivery is not a reporting standard. It is an operating standard. This is the system that gets you there.
